## How a good draft comes about

A draft rests on facts (the brief and answers), the code's conventions and similar features, and decisions. The gaps show up before writing, and one round of questions, each saying why it matters, usually closes them; a second round is for what the answers revealed. Past that point, deciding beats asking.

The user's words are the only source of what they want. Everything you add, such as a value for a case they didn't mention, an extra option or a default, is the team's choice: the user approves it only if it is named as such (on the section's Team chose line, or as an assumed decision), and the Reviewer checks the work against their words, not against your draft. Things you take for true about the code without a quote behind them are marked `[A1] assumed: …`, one per line; the Intern checks each in the code before the draft goes on, and a wrong one comes back to you.

The smallest result that meets the request is the easiest to approve and to build, and exact names (files and locations in a plan, concepts and steps in a what-document) are what make it buildable. A new plan with the same approach as a failed one usually fails the same way, so the reports of the failure are where the next plan starts.
